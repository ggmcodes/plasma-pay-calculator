Garmin vivoactive 5 review

A good fitness watch, and a clear warning about what a wrist sensor cannot tell a plasma donor.

The verdict: The vivoactive 5 is a capable, long-lasting fitness watch with genuinely useful sleep and recovery tracking. Buy it if you want to see whether donation days are costing you sleep or energy over weeks. Do not buy it expecting it to tell you whether you can donate, because it does not measure blood pressure, haematocrit, protein or anything else a centre screens for.

What it does well

  • Eleven days of battery in smartwatch mode means it is actually on your wrist when the data would be useful, rather than on a charger.
  • Sleep scoring and heart rate variability trends are the two measures most likely to show whether a twice-weekly donation schedule is affecting you, viewed over weeks rather than days.
  • Body Battery gives a single readable number for energy, which is easier to act on than a page of metrics.
  • Wheelchair mode tracks pushes instead of steps, which is a real accessibility feature rather than a marketing line.

Where it falls short

  • It does not measure blood pressure. No wrist optical sensor at this price does. If you have been deferred at a centre for a blood pressure reading, this watch will not tell you anything about it and buying one for that reason is money wasted. An upper arm cuff is the tool for that question.
  • It cannot measure haemoglobin, haematocrit or total protein, which are the readings that actually determine whether you donate on a given day. Nothing worn on a wrist can.
  • Wrist heart rate from any optical sensor is an estimate that degrades with movement, cold hands and fit. Treat trends over weeks as meaningful and single readings as indicative.
  • The data is only worth the price if you look at it. Owner reports across this whole category are full of watches worn for a month and then left in a drawer.

What owners report

Published owner reviews consistently praise the battery life and the screen, and treat the sleep and Body Battery features as the reason to buy a Garmin over a cheaper tracker. The most common criticism is the absence of features found on Garmin's more expensive lines, particularly mapping, which buyers sometimes assume is present.

Reports about accuracy follow the pattern of the whole category: steps and sleep duration are regarded as reliable enough to trend, and instantaneous heart rate during hard, jerky movement is regarded as the weak point.

Who it suits

  • Donors curious whether a twice-weekly schedule is affecting their sleep or energy, who will actually read the trend.
  • Anyone who wants a fitness watch and is using plasma income to fund it.
  • People who want long battery life and dislike nightly charging.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone buying it to predict or explain a deferral. Buy an upper arm blood pressure monitor for blood pressure, and take any repeated deferral to your own doctor.
  • Donors who want maps and navigation, which live on Garmin's more expensive models.
  • Anyone who will not open the app. The hardware is only as useful as the attention you give it.

Common questions

Can a Garmin watch tell me if I can donate plasma?

No. Eligibility is decided at the centre by a screening that includes blood pressure, pulse, temperature, haematocrit or haemoglobin and total protein, plus a health history. None of those except pulse can be read from a wrist, and pulse alone decides nothing. Only the centre's staff can tell you whether you can donate today.

Does the vivoactive 5 measure blood pressure?

No. It measures heart rate optically at the wrist. Blood pressure needs a cuff, and the accepted home method is an upper arm cuff. Our comparison at /compare/blood-pressure-monitor-vs-pulse-oximeter explains which device answers which question.

Will it show me whether donating is affecting my sleep?

It will show you a trend, which is the honest answer. Sleep score and heart rate variability across several weeks may show a pattern around donation days. A single night tells you very little. If you feel persistently unwell or exhausted after donating, that is a conversation with your own doctor, not a chart.

Garmin vivoactive 5 vs Fitbit Charge 6: which is better value?

The Charge 6 costs less and shows the same broad trends. The vivoactive has a larger AMOLED screen, more sports apps and longer battery. If the purpose is noticing a pattern rather than training, the cheaper band is enough.

How long does the battery really last?

Garmin publishes up to 11 days in smartwatch mode and about 5 days with the display always on. Owner reports broadly match that, with the always-on display being the single biggest drain.
