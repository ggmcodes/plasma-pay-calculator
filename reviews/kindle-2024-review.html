Kindle review

Light enough for one hand, pages with one thumb, and a battery measured in weeks.

The verdict: The base Kindle is the single best-suited device on this page for a donation chair, because it weighs almost nothing, turns pages with one thumb and does not need a charger in your bag. Buy it if you read and want your donation time back. Skip it if you would rather watch video, in which case a Fire HD 10 costs less and does that properly.

What it does well

  • One hand is all it takes. The device is light enough to hold in the hand that is free, and a tap on either edge turns the page. That single property is why it suits a donation chair better than a phone, a tablet or a console.
  • Six weeks of battery means there is never a charging cable to remember, and never a device that dies halfway through a session.
  • A glare-free screen works under the hard overhead lighting most donation floors use, where a glossy tablet screen reflects the ceiling back at you.
  • The front light and dark mode mean the reading position does not depend on where the chair happens to face.

Where it falls short

  • It only does one thing. There is no video, no browser worth using and no apps. If your donation time is when you catch up on shows, this is the wrong device and you will resent it.
  • This model is not waterproof. The Paperwhite is, and costs less at the time of writing, which makes the base model's price harder to justify unless size and weight are what you are buying.
  • Page refresh on electronic ink is slower than a screen, and it is noticeable if you flick back and forth to check something. For linear reading it is irrelevant.
  • At about $150 it is real money against typical donation pay. If you already own a phone you read on, the honest answer may be that you do not need this at all.

What owners report

Published owner reviews for the base Kindle are consistent about what it is for. People who read regularly describe the weight and the battery as the features they notice daily, and the screen under bright light as the reason they stopped reading on a phone. The most common criticism is that the base model lost waterproofing and a larger screen that the Paperwhite has, at a price that has at times been higher.

Complaints about advertisements on the lock screen are frequent enough to mention: the cheaper listing prices are for the version showing sponsored screens, and removing them costs extra. Check which version you are buying.

Who it suits

  • Donors who read and want their donation time to be reading time rather than dead time.
  • Anyone who dislikes carrying chargers, since this needs one about every six weeks.
  • People bothered by screen glare from overhead lighting on a donation floor.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone whose plan is video or games. Buy the Fire HD 10 instead, or use headphones and an audiobook.
  • Donors who want a waterproof reader, which is the Paperwhite.
  • People who already read comfortably on their phone and would be paying $150 for a small improvement.

Common questions

What is the best thing to bring to a plasma donation?

Something you can use with one hand. One arm is cannulated and needs to stay still, so a device that pages with a single thumb is worth more than a device with more features. An e-reader, a phone or audio through earbuds all work. A games console generally does not.

Kindle vs tablet for a donation centre?

A Kindle is lighter, lasts weeks and does not glare under overhead lights. A tablet plays video and runs apps. Our full comparison at /compare/e-reader-vs-tablet works through battery, weight, glare and cost.

Is the base Kindle or the Paperwhite better?

The Paperwhite has a larger screen, a warm light, waterproofing and longer battery, and it is often cheaper. The base model is smaller and lighter. Unless minimum weight is the point, most people should buy the Paperwhite.

Does the battery really last six weeks?

Amazon publishes up to six weeks, based on modest daily reading with wireless off and a low light setting. Heavy reading with the light high and wireless on is much shorter. For a donor using it twice a week it is realistically months, not weeks.

Can you read while donating plasma?

Yes, and most centres expect it. Keep the cannulated arm still and relaxed, follow whatever squeezing instruction the staff give you, and hold the device in the free hand. If you feel lightheaded, dizzy or unwell at any point, stop and tell staff immediately rather than reading through it.
