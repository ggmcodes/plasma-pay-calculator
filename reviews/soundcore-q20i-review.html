Soundcore Q20i review

Hybrid noise cancelling, 40 hours of battery, and the pair we would actually recommend to most donors.

The verdict: The Q20i is the pair most readers of this site should buy: hybrid noise cancelling, 40 hours of battery, a transparency mode for hearing staff, and a price that is a fraction of a single donation session's worth of premium alternatives. Buy it unless you wear headphones for hours daily. Skip it only if you need the deepest possible cancellation or the best sound quality.

What it does well

  • Forty hours with cancelling on at about $45 is a specification that used to cost four times as much, and for a device used ninety minutes a week that is all the battery anyone needs.
  • Hybrid cancellation with internal and external microphones handles the continuous machine hum of a donation floor, which is the easiest kind of noise to cancel.
  • A dedicated transparency mode, which is how you hear a phlebotomist speaking to you without pulling anything off one handed.
  • If it gets sat on, dropped on a clinic floor or left behind, replacing it costs less than a single donation at most centres. That is a real advantage in a place you visit twice a week.

Where it falls short

  • Cancellation is good for the price, not good in absolute terms. Sudden voices and clattering equipment come through clearly, and only the steady background drops away.
  • The default tuning is bass-heavy, and BassUp makes it more so. For podcasts and audiobooks the app equaliser is close to necessary, which means installing an app.
  • Build is plastic and it feels it. Owner reports about hinges and headband wear are more common than for pairs costing several times as much.
  • It is over-ear, so it has the same reclined-chair problem as every over-ear pair: the cups meet the headrest and can push forward on your ears.

What owners report

Published owner reviews are consistently surprised by the value, and battery life reports match the published figures closely, which is worth noting in a category where they often do not. Sound quality is generally described as good for the money with an emphasis on bass.

The recurring criticisms are build quality and the depth of the noise cancelling. Neither is a defect at this price, and both are exactly what you would expect from a pair a quarter the cost of the well-known alternatives.

Who it suits

  • Donors who want the noise of a centre reduced and are not spending several hundred dollars to do it.
  • Anyone worried about damaging or losing headphones in a busy public setting.
  • People who want long battery life and do not want to think about charging.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone who wears headphones for many hours daily, where a better pair earns its cost.
  • Donors who find over-ear cups uncomfortable when reclined, who want in-ear buds instead.
  • People who will not install an app and dislike bass-forward tuning, since the two go together here.

Common questions

Are cheap noise cancelling headphones good enough for a plasma centre?

For this specific job, yes. Donation floors produce continuous machine noise, which is the easiest kind for any hybrid system to cancel. What cheap cancellation handles badly is sudden voices, and you want to hear those anyway.

Soundcore Q20i vs Sony WH-1000XM5?

The Sony cancels more and sounds better, and it costs more than five times as much. For a device used ninety minutes a week, the Q20i is the defensible purchase and the Sony is the indulgence.

Will the transparency mode let me hear the staff?

Yes, that is exactly what it is for. Switch to it whenever a phlebotomist is working on your arm, and keep music at a level where you can hear someone speaking to you. Some centres ask you to remove headphones during the initial connection, so follow their instruction.

How long does the battery last?

Soundcore publishes 40 hours with cancelling on and 60 hours without. For twice-weekly visits that is months of use between charges rather than weeks.

Earbuds or over-ear for a donation chair?

Over-ear cancels more, earbuds are more comfortable reclined. Our comparison at /compare/wireless-earbuds-vs-over-ear-headphones sets out both, and the answer usually comes down to whether you lie back or sit up.
