LMNT electrolytes review

The highest sodium mix on the shelf, which is the reason to buy it and the reason it does not suit everyone.

The verdict: LMNT is a zero sugar electrolyte mix built around a deliberately high sodium dose, and that single design choice is both its strength and its warning label. Buy it if you sweat heavily, work in heat, or have been advised to increase sodium. Do not buy it if you have high blood pressure or have been deferred on blood pressure, until you have asked your own doctor.

What it does well

  • It contains no sugar, which is the honest advantage over sports drinks for anyone drinking one daily rather than during exercise.
  • The sodium dose is high enough to matter in the situations where sodium genuinely matters: heavy sweating, hot work, and diets that cut processed food sharply.
  • Stick packs travel and need no measuring, so it is a product people actually use rather than a tub that sits open.
  • Flavour reports are strong for a category where most products taste of salt, and taste decides whether anyone drinks it.

Where it falls short

  • Roughly $1.50 per serving is expensive for salt and potassium in water. Cheaper mixes deliver a similar electrolyte load, and for most people water and a normal diet already do.
  • The high sodium is not universally good. Anyone with high blood pressure, heart or kidney conditions, or who has been deferred at a centre on blood pressure, should ask a doctor before adding a gram of sodium a day.
  • It is salty. A substantial share of people who try it dislike the taste and never finish the box, which makes the cost per serving worse than it looks.
  • It does not make you eligible to donate. Hydration helps the process go smoothly and helps you feel better afterwards. Screening measures other things, and only the centre decides.

What owners report

Published owner reviews divide sharply and predictably along the salt line. People who came to it from endurance sport, physical outdoor work or low-carb diets are consistently enthusiastic. People who bought it as a general wellness drink frequently describe it as too salty to finish, and that split is visible across thousands of reviews.

Price is the second consistent theme. Owners who value it accept the cost, and owners who do not point out that electrolyte tablets cost a fraction as much per serving.

Who it suits

  • Donors who work outdoors or in heat and sweat heavily before appointments.
  • Anyone whose doctor has specifically advised increasing sodium intake.
  • People on low-carb or whole-food diets who have cut most dietary sodium without meaning to.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone with high blood pressure, or who has been deferred on a blood pressure reading, without medical advice first.
  • Donors who want the cheapest way to add electrolytes, where tablets cost far less per serving.
  • People who dislike salty drinks, who will not finish the box.

Common questions

Do plasma donors need electrolytes?

Most do not need a supplement. Centres advise drinking plenty of water and eating properly, and a normal diet supplies electrolytes. Mixes matter more if you sweat heavily, work in heat or have been advised to increase sodium. Follow your centre's guidance and your doctor's.

How much sodium is in LMNT?

It is deliberately high compared with most mixes, which is the product's whole design. Read the current nutrition panel on the box you are buying rather than a figure from a web page, since formulations change and this is a number that matters.

LMNT vs Liquid I.V. for plasma donors?

LMNT is higher sodium and sugar free, Liquid I.V. is sweeter with less sodium. Our comparison at /compare/lmnt-vs-liquid-iv-for-plasma-donors sets out the electrolyte content and cost per serving side by side.

Can electrolytes stop me being deferred?

No, and it is worth being direct: deferrals happen for reasons a drink mix does not touch, including blood pressure, pulse, temperature, haematocrit and total protein. Ask the centre what the reading was, and take a repeated deferral to your own doctor.

Is high sodium safe?

It depends entirely on you. For a healthy person sweating heavily it is usually fine. For someone with high blood pressure, or heart or kidney conditions, adding a gram of sodium a day is not a small thing. Ask your doctor before you start, particularly if a centre has already flagged your blood pressure.
