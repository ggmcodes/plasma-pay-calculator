What it does well

  • Forty two grams in a single bottle is a large serving, and it needs no shaker, no water and no washing up.
  • Shelf stable, so a bottle can live in a bag for days without a fridge. That is the difference between a product used and a product forgotten.
  • Lactose free despite being milk based, which suits people who react to lactose but tolerate dairy protein.
  • Milk protein is a complete protein containing all nine essential amino acids, which collagen products are not.

Where it falls short

  • The cost per gram is roughly four times that of a tub of whey powder. You are paying for the bottle and the convenience, and that is a legitimate purchase, but it should be a conscious one.
  • It is dairy. Lactose free is not dairy free, so it is not suitable if you avoid milk protein for allergy or dietary reasons.
  • Ready to drink shakes are calorie dense as well as protein dense. If your overall intake matters to you, read the whole label rather than the protein figure.
  • It will not change a total protein screening result. Total protein reflects your general nutrition and health, and repeated low readings are a matter for your own doctor, not a purchase decision.

What owners report

Published owner reviews are consistently positive on taste and texture, which is the usual failure point for ready to drink shakes, and on the convenience of shelf stability. The two recurring complaints are price and the delivered condition of the bottles, since a 12 pack of liquid is heavy and packaging damage in transit is mentioned regularly.

People who use these describe them as a replacement for skipping a meal rather than as a supplement to a full diet, which is a reasonable way to think about a 42 g bottle.

Who it suits

  • Donors who go straight from work to a centre and will not get a proper meal in between.
  • Anyone who has bought protein powder before and never mixed it.
  • People who want a complete protein and cannot tolerate lactose.

Who should skip it

  • Anyone counting cost per gram, where a 5 lb tub of whey is far cheaper.
  • People avoiding dairy entirely, who need a plant based option.
  • Donors hoping to alter a screening result, which no shake does.

Common questions

Should I drink a protein shake before donating plasma?

Centres generally advise eating a proper meal beforehand and staying hydrated, and a shake can stand in when a meal is not going to happen. It is not a substitute for eating well generally, and it is not a way to influence a screening. Follow your centre's own pre-donation guidance.

Core Power vs protein powder: which is better value?

Powder, by a wide margin per gram. Core Power costs roughly four times as much for the same protein, and you are buying the bottle, the shelf stability and never having to mix anything. That is a fair trade if it means you actually take it.

Protein powder or protein bars?

Powder is cheapest, bars are the most portable, shakes sit between them on both counts. The comparison at /compare/protein-powder-vs-protein-bars runs the numbers and is honest about which one people keep using.

Is it lactose free?

Yes, Core Power publishes it as lactose free, but it is made from ultra-filtered milk, so it still contains milk protein. It is not suitable if you avoid dairy for allergy reasons. Check with your doctor if you are unsure.

Will more protein stop me being deferred?

Do not treat a screening as an obstacle. Total protein is measured to protect you, and a low result can reflect nutrition or an underlying health issue. Ask the centre what your reading was and take a persistent low result to your own doctor.
