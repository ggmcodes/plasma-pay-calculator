LMNT vs Liquid IV

Two electrolyte powders, and whether a donor needs either one

Verdict

Neither is required. Water plus normally salted food covers most donors, and both of these are convenience products rather than necessities. If you want one, LMNT carries far more sodium per serving with no sugar, which suits someone who sweats heavily or eats a low-sodium diet. Liquid IV carries less sodium plus sugar, which some people find easier to drink in volume. Whether either helps you pass the protein and hematocrit checks at your centre is a question for the centre staff and your own doctor, not for a drink mix.

Plasma is mostly water, and donation removes a meaningful volume of fluid, which is why every centre tells donors to drink before they arrive. That advice is free. Electrolyte powders are a paid version of it, and whether they add anything over water and ordinary food depends on you rather than on the marketing.

Choose the LMNT Zero Sugar Electrolytes, 30 Count if…

Choose the higher-sodium option if you sweat heavily, work outdoors, or generally eat very little salt, and if you have no medical reason to limit sodium. Mix it in the volume of water the label specifies rather than in a small amount, because a concentrated salty drink on an empty stomach is unpleasant and can be counterproductive. Start it the day before, not fifteen minutes before you walk in, since fluid takes hours to distribute.

Who decides what you should be taking

This site is written by people who track plasma centre pay rates, not clinicians, and nothing here is medical advice. Sodium intake is a genuine medical question for anyone with high blood pressure, kidney disease, heart failure or a diet their doctor has set, so ask your own doctor before adding a high-sodium product to your day. On donation day, the people who decide whether you are fit to donate are the screening staff at the centre, using the checks the FDA requires. Tell them what you are taking, tell them how you feel, and follow their pre-donation instructions rather than a general article.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much water should I drink before donating plasma?

Most centres ask donors to drink a substantial amount of water in the day before and the hours before donating, and each centre publishes its own figure. Follow the number your centre gives rather than a general one, because they know their process. Spreading intake across the day works better than drinking a large volume immediately beforehand, which mostly ends in a bathroom trip.

Do electrolytes help you pass the protein test?

No. The protein check measures total serum protein, which comes from what you eat over days and weeks rather than from a drink mix. Electrolyte powders address fluid and salt balance, not protein. If you are being deferred for low protein, that is a conversation to have with the centre staff and with your own doctor rather than something to solve at the supplement shelf.

Can you drink too much water before donating?

Yes. Drinking very large volumes of plain water in a short time can dilute blood sodium, which is a genuine medical risk, and it is not made safer by the fact that donating is a good reason to hydrate. Follow the centre's guidance on quantity and timing, spread it out, and if you feel unwell at any point tell the staff.

Will hydration stop me feeling dizzy after donating?

Being well hydrated and having eaten beforehand reduces the chance of a fainting episode, which is one of the more common reactions. It does not eliminate it. If you feel light-headed, hot, nauseated or sweaty during or after a donation, tell staff immediately and stay seated rather than trying to walk it off.
