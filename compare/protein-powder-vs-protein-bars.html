Protein Powder vs Protein Bars

Cost per gram against the thing you will actually carry

Verdict

Powder wins clearly on cost per gram of protein, often by a factor of three or four, and it is the sensible base for anyone donating regularly. Bars win on convenience, and a bar in a bag beats a shaker you left at home. Most regular donors end up with both: powder at home, a bar in the car for the waiting room. Watch the sugar alcohols in bars, which cause bloating and stomach upset in a fair number of people.

Regular plasma donation is a protein-hungry routine, and both of these are ways to add protein without cooking. They differ mainly on price and on whether the protein actually gets eaten.

What eating well does and does not do

Eating well supports how you feel through a regular donation schedule, and centres routinely advise a protein-rich diet and a proper meal before donating. It is not a way to change a screening outcome on demand, and no food or supplement makes anyone eligible to donate. The screening exists to protect both the donor and the patient who receives the plasma. If your readings are drifting or you are being deferred, ask the centre staff what the numbers were and take that to your own doctor. Frequently Asked Questions

What should I eat before donating plasma?

Centres generally advise a proper meal with protein a few hours beforehand, plenty of water, and specifically avoiding fatty food, because fat in the blood makes plasma cloudy and can cause a deferral on the day. Follow the guidance your own centre publishes, since they are the ones assessing the sample.

Are protein bars a good pre-donation snack?

They are convenient and better than nothing, and many are also high in fat, which is the one thing centres ask donors to avoid before donating. Check the fat content on the label as well as the protein. A bar afterwards, alongside the snack most centres provide, is less complicated.

Why do protein bars upset my stomach?

Usually the sugar alcohols, commonly erythritol, maltitol or sorbitol, which are poorly absorbed and ferment in the gut. Fibre content in some bars adds to it. Try a bar with a different sweetener system before giving up on the category, and if stomach symptoms persist beyond that, mention it to your doctor.

Is it worth buying protein powder if I only donate occasionally?

Probably not. The cost advantage of powder comes from volume, and a large tub bought for occasional use tends to go stale in a cupboard. For occasional donors, ordinary food with protein in it does the job and costs nothing extra.
